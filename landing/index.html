The problem

Every dictation tool solves the wrong half of this

You are deep in a file. You remember you need to tell someone something. So you leave: Cmd+Tab to WhatsApp, scroll a list of chats to find the right one, type a sentence you could have said out loud in two seconds, then get pulled into three unread messages you did not come for.

The typing speed was never the problem. SuperWhisper, Wispr Flow, and macOS Dictation are all fast at turning speech into text. None of them know who you are talking to. You still have to open WhatsApp, still have to find the chat, still have to leave what you were doing.

And the tools that do get you typing faster introduce a new failure: they quietly rewrite what you said. Real users hitting this on Apple's own support forum, about a dictation tool changing their words after correctly hearing them:

“dictation gets my words correct but then it decides I must mean something else”

andersonkl, Apple Support Communities

Why it is different

Two rules, both structural, not marketing

These are not settings you can turn off by accident. They are how the code is built.

It never rewrites your words

The message body is sliced character for character out of what you actually said. Removing a stray "um" is allowed. Rewriting a sentence into something you did not say is architecturally impossible, because there is no rewriting step in the code at all. This also means Hinglish just works: byte offsets do not care what language you are speaking.

“some kind of stupid algorithm that is changing my words after they record correctly”

andersonkl, Apple Support Communities, on a competing dictation tool

It never guesses a recipient

One clear match resolves and Voicy moves on. Two close matches, like two contacts named Rahul, and it asks instead of picking one. A wrong send on WhatsApp cannot be undone, so the app is built to stop and ask rather than guess. Correct a match once and it remembers the mapping for next time.

Fully on-device

Transcription runs on Apple's on-device Speech framework. Contact matching runs against your local address book. Nothing is uploaded to make either of those work. Some competitors take the opposite approach:

“In their quest for ‘context awareness,’ Wispr Flow captures screenshots of your active window to ‘understand’ what you are doing.”

Ryan Shrott, Medium

Zero ban risk, because no WhatsApp API is touched

WhatsApp automation libraries reimplement its private protocol and get accounts permanently banned, sometimes after a handful of messages. Voicy never speaks to WhatsApp's servers. It hands macOS a whatsapp://send link, the same click-to-chat mechanism WhatsApp publishes for websites to use, which opens the real app with your message typed in. There is nothing unusual to detect, because nothing unusual happened.

If someone sends you a four-minute voice note while you are on your Mac, you still have to stop and listen to all four minutes. Privacy

Plain statements, not a policy page

Every line below is something you can check yourself by reading the source.

  • Your voice never leaves your Mac.

    Transcription runs on-device with Apple's Speech framework. There is no server and no API key. Airplane mode changes nothing.

  • No message content is ever stored or logged.

    The only thing saved is a name mapping, such as "Pulkit" pointing to a contact. Never the content of a message.

  • Read-only on the WhatsApp folder.

    The voice-note module reads files that already exist on your disk. It does not write to, move, or delete anything WhatsApp owns.

  • Your contacts are never uploaded.

    They are read into memory to match a spoken name against, and that is the extent of it.

  • The microphone opens when you hold the key and closes when you let go.

    No background listening, no screen recording, no keylogging.

Permissions

Two permissions up front, nothing else

Most tools in this category ask for Accessibility access before you have seen them do anything. Voicy asks for two things on first launch, and it is fully usable with just those two.

Microphone

To hear you when you hold the hotkey.

Contacts

To turn a spoken name into a phone number.

It works fully without either toggle: hotkey, transcription, contact matching, and the message pre-filled and ready in WhatsApp. You opt into each upgrade after you trust the app, not before you have tried it.

FAQ

Questions worth answering plainly

Will this get my WhatsApp banned?

No API is involved, so there is nothing for WhatsApp to detect as automation. Voicy hands macOS a whatsapp://send link, the same click-to-chat mechanism WhatsApp publishes for websites, which opens the real app with your message typed in. From WhatsApp's point of view, a real person opened the real app and pressed a real key.

Does my audio ever leave my Mac?

No. Transcription runs on Apple's on-device Speech framework. There is no server, no API key, and no upload step in the code.

Does it work offline?

Yes, for the hotkey, transcription, and contact matching, since all three run on-device. Sending still needs WhatsApp Desktop itself to be able to deliver the message, the same as if you typed it yourself.

What about Indian English and Hinglish?

Indian names are genuinely hard for every speech engine. Voicy loads your contacts before you speak and biases the recognizer toward the roughly 100 names that actually matter to you, instead of guessing from the whole English language. Because the message body is sliced by character position rather than regenerated, Hinglish sentences pass through untouched too, since byte offsets do not care what language you are speaking.

What macOS version do I need?

macOS 26 or later, on Apple Silicon, with WhatsApp Desktop installed. Only WhatsApp is wired up. Telegram and iMessage are not supported, and there is no Windows, Linux, or iOS build.

Is it free and open source?

Yes, MIT licensed on GitHub, built with zero third-party dependencies. There is no subscription and no download link, because there is no signed release yet: you clone the repository and build it yourself. That also matches what people already ask for by name in the on-device dictation community:
